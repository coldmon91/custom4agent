# Explanation

**Explanation is a discursive treatment of a subject that permits reflection. Understanding-oriented.**

Explanation deepens and broadens understanding. It brings clarity, light and context, and serves the user's **study** rather than their work.

**Reflection** is the key concept: it occurs *after* something else and depends on it, yet shines a new light on it.

Explanation's perspective is higher and wider than the other three. Not the user's eye-level view of a how-to guide, nor the close-up of reference. Its scope is a *topic* — an area of knowledge that has to be bounded in some reasonable, meaningful way.

For the user, explanation joins things together. It answers *Can you tell me about ...?*
It's the only kind of documentation that makes sense to read away from the product.

Names other than *Explanation* work fine: *Discussion*, *Background*, *Conceptual guides*, *Topics*.

## The Value and Place of Explanation

Explanation is characterised by its **distance from the practitioner's active concerns**. It has no direct implication for what they do right now, which is why it's sometimes treated as less important. That's a mistake: it may be less *urgent* than the other three, but it's no less *important*, and it isn't a luxury. No practitioner of a craft can afford to be without an understanding of that craft.

*Explanation* means unfolding — revealing what is hidden in the folds. *Understanding* shares roots with holding and grasping (*comprehend*). Understanding doesn't *come from* explanation, but explanation forms the web that holds everything together. Without it a practitioner's knowledge is loose, fragmented and fragile, and their practice is **anxious**.

### Boundaries

Explanation is often not explicitly recognised in documentation at all; instead it gets scattered in small parcels across other sections.

It's also hard to write. Where does one start, and where conclude? Its open-endedness gives the writer too many possibilities. Tutorials, how-to guides and reference all have their scope fixed by something well-defined — what the user must learn, what task they must achieve, or the extent of the machinery itself. Explanation has none of that.

It helps to have a real or imagined **why question** as a prompt. Otherwise, draw lines around a reasonable area and be satisfied with that.

## Writing Good Explanation

- **Make connections.** You're weaving a web of understanding. Connect to other things, even outside the immediate topic, if it helps.
- **Provide context.** Explain *why* things are so — design decisions, historical reasons, technical constraints. Draw implications; mention specific examples.
- **Talk *about* the subject.** Explanation is *around* its topic. Titles should reflect that — you should be able to put an implicit (or explicit) *about* in front of each one: *About user authentication*, *About database connection policies*. Things worth discussing: the bigger picture, history, choices and alternatives, reasons and justifications.
- **Admit opinion and perspective.** All human activity and knowledge is invested with opinion, and the reality of any human creation is rich with it. Any understanding also comes from a standpoint — which means other standpoints exist. **Explanation can and must consider alternatives**, counter-examples, and multiple approaches to the same question. Think of it as discussion, which can weigh up contrary opinions.
- **Keep it closely bounded.** Explanation tends to absorb other things: intent on covering the topic, the writer feels the urge to add instruction and technical description. Documentation already has places for those, and letting them creep in both interferes with the explanation and removes them from view where they belong.

## The Language of Explanation

| Pattern | Purpose |
|---|---|
| *The reason for x is because historically, y ...* | Explain |
| *W is better than z, because ...* | Offer judgements and even opinions where appropriate |
| *An x in system y is analogous to a w in system z. However ...* | Provide context that helps the reader |
| *Some users prefer w (because z). This can be a good approach, but...* | Weigh up alternatives |
| *An x interacts with a y as follows: ...* | Unfold the machinery's internals, to help understand why it does what it does |

## Analogy

Harold McGee's *On Food and Cooking* (1984). It teaches you to cook nothing, contains no recipes, and is not a work of reference. It places food and cooking in the context of history, society, science and technology — why we do what we do in the kitchen, and how that changed.

Clearly not a book to read *while* cooking; you read it when you want to reflect on cooking. It illuminates the subject from multiple angles. Afterwards your understanding is changed — maybe not immediately applicable next time you're in the kitchen, but it changes how you think about your craft, and it will affect your practice.
