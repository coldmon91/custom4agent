# Diátaxis as a Guide to Work

Diátaxis guides not only documentation *content* but documentation *process*.

Documentation is rarely delivered once, complete and final. Usually it's ongoing — developed alongside a product that itself keeps changing — and often the job is remediating a body of work someone else left behind.

Diátaxis runs counter to much accepted wisdom here: **it discourages planning and top-down workflows**, preferring small, responsive iterations from which the overall pattern emerges.

## Use Diátaxis as a Guide, Not a Plan

Diátaxis describes a complete picture of documentation, but that structure is **not a plan** you must complete. It's a **guide** — a map to check that you're in the right place and heading the right way.

Its point is to give you a way to think about your documentation, so you can make sense of what it's doing and what you're trying to do with it: assess it, locate the problems, judge what would improve it.

## Don't Worry About Structure

Structure is key to documentation, and **using Diátaxis means not spending energy trying to get the structure correct**.

Keep following the prompts and your documentation will eventually assume the Diátaxis structure — but it will have assumed that shape *because* it was improved. Not the reverse. Structure imposed from outside doesn't improve anything.

Getting started does not mean dividing your docs into four sections. **It certainly does not mean creating empty tutorials/how-to/reference/explanation sections with nothing in them. Don't do that. It's horrible.**

Instead, make changes where you see opportunities, so the documentation starts to take a certain shape. At some point the changes will appear to *demand* that material move under a particular heading — and that's how the top-level structure forms. **Diátaxis changes the structure of your documentation from the inside.**

## Work One Step at a Time

Diátaxis prescribes a structure strongly, but whatever state your docs are in — even a complete mess by any standard — it's always possible to improve them **iteratively**.

It's natural to want to finish a large tranche before publishing, so you have something substantial to show. Avoid it: **every step in the right direction is worth publishing immediately**.

And although Diátaxis provides a big picture, **don't try to work on the big picture**. It's both unnecessary and unhelpful. Diátaxis is designed to guide small steps; keep taking small steps to arrive where you want to go.

## Just Do Something

Tidying a huge mess tempts you to tear it down and start again. Avoid that too. You don't even need to seek out things to improve:

1. **Choose something** — any piece of the documentation. If you don't already have something you know you want to put right, *don't go looking for outstanding problems*. Take what's in front of you: the file you're in, the last page you read. If there isn't one, choose literally at random.

2. **Assess it.** Consider it critically. Preferably something small — no bigger than a page, better still a paragraph or a sentence. Challenge it against Diátaxis standards:
   - What user need is represented by this?
   - How well does it serve that need?
   - What can be added, moved, removed or changed to serve it better?
   - Do its language and logic meet the requirements of this mode of documentation?

3. **Decide what to do.** Based on those answers: *what single next action will produce an immediate improvement here?*

4. **Do it.** Complete that single action and **consider it completed** — publish it, or at least commit the change. Don't feel you need to do anything else for it to be a worthy improvement.

Then go back to the beginning.

Working this way removes the most paralysing part of a documentation writer's job — working out what to do. It keeps work flowing toward the desired end without spending energy on a plan.

## Let the Work Develop Organically

### Well-formed organic growth

Organic growth happens at the cellular level. The structure of the organism as a whole is guaranteed by healthy development of cells, each according to rules appropriate to its kind — not by a structure imposed from above or outside. **Good structure develops from within.**

Same with documentation: follow the principles, and it attains a healthy structure because its internal components are themselves well-formed — built from the inside out, one cell at a time.

### Complete, not finished

A plant is **never finished** — it can always develop further. But at every stage, from seed to mature tree, it is **always complete**: nothing is missing, and it's in a state appropriate to its stage of development.

Documentation is never finished either, because it must keep adapting to the product and to users' needs. But it can always be complete: useful to users, appropriate to its current stage, structurally healthy, and ready for the next stage.
